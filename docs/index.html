Part 1

The opportunity

What is already moving, and why now is the moment.

01 · The opportunity

Local energy is already moving. Connected, it moves faster.

Communities, cities, networks, grid operators and research centres in Canada, Europe and beyond are building local energy right now, and each brings real strengths. The Alliance connects them around four strengths: shared tools and data structures so that work builds on work, a trusted table with leadership so that projects start, a science-to-action pipeline so that research reaches real projects, and advocacy so that local energy is heard.

Tools that work together

Great tools exist for design, operation and monitoring. Built on shared data structures they connect, and every case becomes comparable.

Leadership at the table

City, utility, financiers, developers and neighbours around one project, with a trusted panel of experts leading and guiding the process until it advances.

Science that reaches action

Research centres and real projects bid together, so methods are tested where they matter and projects get the best science.

Advocacy

Communities, cities and grid operators speaking together, with evidence, carry further.

02 · Why now

Policy, money and technology are lining up on the local level.

Three shifts make local energy the place where the transition is won or lost, and make the next few years a rare window to organise. The same questions arise in the Global South, where microgrids and community-run systems are often the first access to electricity.

9 → 90 GW
Policy. The European Commission's 2026 Citizens Energy Package aims for roughly a tenfold rise in community-owned renewables by 2030. It is voluntary, so delivery depends on capacity on the ground.
8,000+
Scale. Energy communities counted in the EU by the Commission (an academic mapping counts about 3,900: definitions differ). Cities, regions and grid operators are moving too.
Twins
Technology and funding. EU programmes already fund digital twins on the grid side (e.g. TwinEU, DSO4DT) and community facilities separately. The Alliance would build the bridge between them.

Sources: European Commission, REScoop.eu, mapping study (Sustainability 2023), project sites. See references.

03 · Great tools, one shared foundation

Great tools already exist. A shared foundation lets them work together.

We reviewed 35 tools and tool families for managing energy communities and local energy systems, from research projects, networks, public bodies and companies. They cover planning, set-up, energy sharing and virtual metering, aggregation and flexibility, and monitoring. Each has its own front end, back end, data model and indicators, which is where a shared core helps most. For 29 of them we could not find the data model documented in public sources. Tools for off-grid and mini-grid settings in the Global South are only starting to be covered.

Different front ends are fine

Users differ, so interfaces should too.

One foundation saves effort

Data structure, data capture and indicators built once, not in every tool, so tools integrate and cases compare.

A shared core makes tools last

If a tool disappears or two tools join, data and indicators keep their meaning.

04 · The proposal

Four strengths, one alliance.

The Alliance is where four strengths meet. Digital twins are the shared language and the first thing we build, but the Alliance is about what organisations can do together. Select a strength.

1

Shared tools and data structures

Open building blocks that make every tool and every case work together.

2

Facilitation and leadership

A neutral table, and someone to lead it: a panel of experts that guides the process until things advance.

3

Science-to-action pipeline

Research and real projects together: turning science and ideas into funded action, with the right partners.

4

Advocacy

Credible, evidence-based advocacy towards the EU, the IEA, the OECD and city networks.

Alongside the four jobs: technology transfer and training with the Global South

Members with experience in rural microgrids and energy access (Azimut 360 and Aiguasol in sub-Saharan Africa) would bring that practice into the Alliance. In return, open tools, case records and training would be adapted and transferred to southern partners, with local organisations leading the adaptation. It cuts across the four jobs: the commons must also fit off-grid cases, the expert panel can lead local processes, the pipeline can reach development and climate funding, and advocacy can carry North-South evidence.

06 · Already doing it

Bringing people to the table, and leading them to a decision, is something we already do.

Our centre has played the intermediary in two district heating initiatives and has started two projects that bring the stakeholders of a sector together. In each case someone had to lead and guide the process, or it would not have advanced. We would make this one of the Alliance's essential activities, with more partners and at a larger scale.

District heating at Laval and at Concordia

We acted as the intermediary between the partners, running workshops and working out the different problems of the neighbours.

ABC Accelerator (Montréal, since 2022)

A "sustainability SWAT team" of the city, a utility, a bank, developers, engineers, architects, lawyers and the university works on real projects. Two pilots, over 200 hours each, a three-month process.

CÔTÉ Habitat (Montréal)

A centre that brings together a housing network, a transition alliance and Concordia around the transition of existing housing: technical, social, urban and financial support in one process. Under development, looking for a salaried lead.

What we learned

A university as neutral convener builds trust. A concrete project beats an abstract debate. With the whole chain at one table, barriers become opportunities. Without leadership and guidance, the intermediary role is lost and things stop. It takes three to four months and a funded coordinator.

The Alliance would lead, not only host

A panel of experts drawn from the different member teams would act as the leaders and intermediaries of these processes, to support the transition to local systems. The Alliance offers not only the table, but the leadership of how the processes are applied. The same approach can support local processes in the Global South, with local leads.

07 · The first engine

A new IEA EBC Annex is already on the table, supported by NRCan.

In early October 2026 we submitted a proposal to the IEA EBC Executive Committee for a new Annex: Enabling implementation of Community Energy Solutions through digital platforms. It moves the question from "can this work?" to "who will make it work?", using digital twins for fast feasibility and adding governance, financing and regulation to the model.

What it does

Governance models, financing, regulation and open-source digital-twin tools, brought together in one platform that guides a project from pre-feasibility to implementation.

How long

1 year of preparation, 3 of work and 1 of reporting, if the ExCo approves (decision expected at its November 2026 meeting).

Why an Alliance too

An Annex is government-led and ends. The Alliance keeps the shared core, tools and partners alive afterwards, and lets companies and community networks take part.

Proposed lead and Operating Agent: Ursula Eicker (Concordia). Proposed outside collaborators include FCM, the Global Covenant of Mayors, C40, UN-Habitat and the OECD. Details in the annex ↓

09 · How it is run

Neutral by design, so competitors can sit at the same table.

A short charter

  • Open by default for the shared core
  • Members keep their proprietary work
  • No services that compete with members
  • Competition-law guidance for all meetings

Balanced bodies

  • Assembly of all members
  • Board balanced across the six member families
  • Technical committee for the commons
  • Panel of experts to lead facilitated processes
  • Small secretariat

Fair play on projects

  • Lead roles rotate with clear criteria
  • The Alliance facilitates, never picks winners
  • A share of won projects returns to the commons (if funders allow)

Legal form starts as a Spanish association (fast and low cost) and could move to an international non-profit when membership and funding justify it.

Annex A · The IEA EBC Annex proposal

The Annex is the first engine: from "can it work?" to "who will make it work?"

Proposed title: Enabling implementation of Community Energy Solutions through digital platforms. The concept was submitted to the IEA EBC Executive Committee in early October 2026, supported by NRCan. Proposed lead and Operating Agent: Ursula Eicker (Concordia University).

The problem it addresses

Community-scale energy systems (solar PV energy communities, district heating and cooling, mixed-energy districts and campuses) are technically mature, yet uptake is slow. Feasibility studies answer "can this work?" but rarely "who will make this work?", so sound projects stall between planning and implementation. The Annex uses digital twins for fast technical and financial assessment and treats governance, participation, ownership, financing and regulation as part of the model.

Six objectives

1. Governance structures

Identify successful stakeholder roles, ownership and operating structures, utility engagement, participation and risk allocation.

2. The implementation gap

Find out why feasible projects do not advance, and build frameworks for institutional barriers, coordination, procurement and acceptance.

3. Financing

Business models, public-private and utility partnerships, community ownership and risk-sharing.

4. Regulation

Compare frameworks for district energy, energy communities, electricity sharing, tariffs, building design and zoning.

5. Digital planning tools

Apply and scale open-source digital-twin tools for demand simulation, community energy mapping, technology screening and life-cycle cost, with regulatory, financing and governance constraints built in.

6. Implementation roadmaps

Step-by-step guidance for municipalities, developers and citizen organisations, from feasibility study to operation.

Outputs

A governance and stakeholder framework, a participation and co-creation framework, a financing and business-model guidebook, a regulatory assessment, implementation roadmaps, demonstration case studies and policy recommendations, all integrated in one digital platform that guides the whole planning process.

Builds on earlier EBC work

Annex 51 (Energy Efficient Communities), Annex 63 (Implementation of Energy Strategies in Communities), Annex 73 (Net Zero Energy Resilient Public Communities), Annex 75 (Cost-effective Building Renovation at District Level) and Annex 83 (Positive Energy Districts). None of them combined fast digital feasibility assessment with the organisational delivery problem.

Timeline and partners

Duration

Preparation 1 year, working phase 3 years, reporting 1 year. Start depends on the ExCo decision.

Coordination

With the DHC, Cities, SHC, Energy Storage and Users (DSM) Technology Collaboration Programmes.

Outside collaborators proposed

Municipal organisations such as FCM, the Global Covenant of Mayors, C40 and Transition Accelerator organisations, and international bodies such as UN-Habitat and the OECD's Cities, Urban Policies and Sustainable Development Division.

How the Alliance relates to it

The Annex is government-led and time-limited. The Alliance would be its permanent, wider home: it keeps the shared core, the tools and the partners alive after the Annex ends, and lets companies and community networks take part, which an Annex cannot do.

Source: EBC Project Concept dated 24 August 2026. Open item: a Technology Readiness Assessment has not been carried out yet; the ExCo would ask for it at the workshop that develops the full proposal.

Annex B · Facilitation: method and track record

How the Alliance would run a facilitated process.

Local energy projects move when the city, the utility, the financier, the developer and the neighbours work out together who does what, and someone leads them to a decision. A neutral convener with leadership makes that happen.

Track record

District heating at Laval and at Concordia

Our centre played the intermediary between the partners, ran workshops and worked out the different problems of the neighbours.

ABC Accelerator

Started in 2022 within the Montréal climate partnership. A "sustainability SWAT team" of seven kinds of actors: university, developer, financing, utility, engineering and architecture, law and the city. First test with two real developers.

CÔTÉ Habitat

A centre for the ecological transition of existing housing in Montréal, led by a housing network, a transition alliance and Concordia. Technical, social, urban and financial support in one process; two pilots planned to test the assumptions and the business models.

The ABC Accelerator in numbers

7
kinds of actors at one table
2
pilot projects with real developers
200+
expert hours per project, plus about 400 hours to build reusable tools
3 months

Estimated emission reductions for the two pilots: about 19% with the baseline proposals and 33% with the next-generation proposals (the project's own estimates, not measured results).

A panel of experts that leads

The table is not enough: without leadership and guidance the intermediary role is lost and nothing advances. The Alliance would form a panel to provide that leadership. This is a proposal, still to be designed.

Who

Experts from the different member teams (technical, social, financial, legal, urban), balanced across the member families, including members with experience in the Global South.

What they do

Lead and guide a local transition process, keep the intermediary role intact and make sure it moves towards a decision.

How it is assigned

A lead or team per process, rotating, with a clear rule when a member has its own commercial interest in the outcome.

What supports them

A small secretariat, shared templates and a funded coordinator, so leadership does not depend on free time.

A process in six steps

1. Start from a concrete project

A real project or place, not an abstract topic.

2. Invite the whole chain

City, utility, financier, developer, engineers and architects, legal advice, and the community or neighbours. In off-grid projects, also the mini-grid operator and the local authority.

3. Agree the rules first

Roles, confidentiality, what can be shared, and a fair way to select who takes part.

4. Listen and map

Workshops to understand each party's problems and constraints, and who is most and least open to change.

5. Work in small commissions

For example public space, energy systems, buildings and certification, with a monthly plenary.

6. Document and hand back

A diagnosis, proposals at two levels of ambition, ways to fund them and recommendations with costs and impacts, plus templates others can reuse.

What we learned

  • A university acting as a central, neutral agent is seen very positively and builds trust.
  • Innovative options scare people less when banks, municipalities and key actors visibly back them.
  • Examples from others, and a good pitch, speed up decisions.
  • Technical teams can start as the conservative force. Meeting after meeting, with their fears addressed, they became collaborative.
  • Three to four months of process help to smooth the first doubts.
  • Confidentiality worries come first: a non-disclosure agreement with the convener and side agreements with each partner were needed.
  • Without leadership and guidance the intermediary role is lost. In the ABC Accelerator the university coordinated every step; CÔTÉ plans a salaried lead.
  • Free expert time does not allow deep dedication. The coordination needs funding: CÔTÉ plans a salaried lead from the start.

The line with what members do

The Alliance does
  • Lead and guide the process with a panel of experts from member teams
  • Convene, run workshops and facilitate
  • Map problems and agree the rules
  • Coordinate working groups and document the result
  • Turn what is learned into templates and tools open to all
The Alliance does not
  • Design the project or sell engineering
  • Take a commercial role in delivery
  • Choose vendors or winners
  • Replace the expertise members bring to the table

How it connects to the rest

Each process produces a real case for the shared case-record standard, evidence for the white paper and the Annex's governance work, and a pipeline of projects that are ready to be funded. In the Global South, each process could also serve as a training case for local partners.

The shared core: five layers developed once, used by all

Not a platform and not a database: a common structure and vocabulary that any tool can read and write.

1. Geospatial and building metadata

Building identity and footprint, use, age, height, systems, coordinate reference, links to cadastre and open building datasets.

2. Socioeconomic structure

Households, ownership and membership, vulnerability, tariffs and costs, described with the same fields and codes.

3. Data capture and provenance

What is collected, how, by whom and when. Each value says if it was measured, modelled or declared, and where it came from.

4. Real-case records

A standard record for a community or district case study so that different projects can be compared like with like.

5. Key indicators

Agreed definitions, formulas, units and boundaries for the indicators, so numbers from different tools mean the same thing.

If a tool disappears

The data survives in a structure others can read, instead of being lost with the tool.

If two tools want to join

Integration is mapping to the same core once, not rewriting every connector.

If a community compares cases

Real cases are described with the same fields and indicators, so they can be compared.

If you want to keep your own approach

You keep your front end, back end and extras. Exchange within the association is optional.

Core versus yours

Core (shared, versioned, open): ontology, capture and provenance rules, indicator definitions, case-record format.

Yours: front ends, back ends, algorithms, local extras (with a clear prefix), and whatever you choose to exchange.

Annex D · Lessons from other initiatives

What earlier projects already taught us.

Energy sharing, aggregation and community monitoring have been tried in many places. The same lessons come back. They shape what the Alliance should and should not do.

1. Scaling is harder than technology

Local trading and sharing platforms worked in pilots. Growing them hit regulation, billing and member engagement before it hit technology.

Evidence: Reviews of peer-to-peer platforms; Brooklyn Microgrid's main early difficulty was selling energy through a utility bill without being regulated as a utility.

For the Alliance: Share regulatory and billing know-how between members, not only code.

2. Flexibility income alone rarely pays

Revenue from shifting or shaving load is small next to the effort. It works when it sits inside a wider business case.

Evidence: Project LEO (Oxfordshire, 2019 to 2023) concluded that flexibility income must be part of a broader case.

For the Alliance: Position aggregation tools as one service among several, and compare business cases with the same indicators.

3. Data standards come first

Standardisation, automation and investment in data were the main enablers. Without them every new community starts from zero.

Evidence: Project LEO final report; Austrian smart-meter data access (market communication platform, EDDIE open source, ISEG interface, QODEC data quality).

For the Alliance: This is the case for the shared core: ontology, data capture and provenance.

4. Allocation rules are set by regulators and grid operators

How energy is split between members depends on national rules and on the operator's willingness to apply them. Many countries are still on static coefficients.

Evidence: Portugal: fixed, proportional, hierarchical and dynamic coefficients, with an approved pilot of dynamic ones. Spain: usually static coefficients and a 2 km radius. Germany: energy sharing legal from 1 June 2026, but no binding allocation-key rules yet and few operators offer building-level supply.

For the Alliance: Keep a comparative table of rules and of operator practice. It is cheap to maintain and valuable to every member.

5. Residents need a story, not a tariff

People respond to a clear reason and a trusted messenger. Repeat engagement with trading apps is weak.

Evidence: Project LEO (learning how to talk to residents about flexibility); reviews of peer-to-peer platforms; H2020 social engagement lessons.

For the Alliance: This is where facilitation and the panel of experts add value that software does not.

6. Benefits are rarely evaluated, and never the same way

Few initiatives measure outcomes empirically, and those that do use different indicators, so results cannot be compared.

Evidence: Reviews of peer-to-peer pilots; Canada's Smart Energy Communities Benchmark is a self-assessment of ten indicators for nine pilot municipalities; an academic 25-indicator set exists but is not widely used.

For the Alliance: Publish a minimal common indicator set and a case record, and ask members to report against it.

7. Cooperatives are moving into aggregation

Energy cooperatives are exploring the aggregator role to value their members' flexibility.

Evidence: REScoopVPP (about EUR 4 million, 2020 to 2023, pilots in Belgium, France, Germany, Spain and the UK); Enercoop became a balance responsible party; Som Energia has studied becoming an aggregator.

For the Alliance: Aggregation is a member activity. The Alliance should document and compare it, not run it.

8. Southern settings add logistics, security and capacity risks

Projects in sub-Saharan Africa meet limits in logistics, security and local capacity that rarely show in northern case studies.

Evidence: Azimut 360 and Aiguasol's description of their work in the region.

For the Alliance: Training and local partners are part of any technology transfer, not an afterthought.

9. Intermediaries are what make local projects start

A neutral convenor with enough authority to lead moves projects that stall between stakeholders.

Evidence: Our own record: district heating at Laval and Concordia, ABC Accelerator, CÔTÉ (see the facilitation chapter).

For the Alliance: Keep leadership of the process inside the offer, next to the tools.

A starting point for common indicators

To compare communities, the first step is to agree on a small shared set grouped like the published frameworks. This is a proposal for discussion, to be tested with members.

Energy

Self-consumption, self-sufficiency, share of demand matched locally, peak demand and its reduction, flexibility delivered.

Economic

Cost per member, savings, revenue from sharing and flexibility, investment per kW, payback.

Social

Members, share of vulnerable households, participation and retention, governance indicators.

Environmental

Avoided emissions, local renewable share, and the method used to calculate them.

Capacity (from the Canadian benchmark)

Governance, staff, data, financing and strategy of the municipality or community.

Data quality

For each value: measured, modelled or declared; source; period; resolution.

What we could not find

  • A verified percentage of peak reduction for a real energy community aggregation pilot; the sources found describe designs and targets.
  • Documented allocation tools from Spanish cooperatives and Canadian pilots beyond the regulatory schemes; to be confirmed with members.
  • The data models behind most of the tools above. Vendor claims in the tool table are marked as such and should be treated as unverified.
  • The outcome of Brooklyn Microgrid after its pilot years.

Annex G · Partners in the Global South

A bridge to countries where microgrids and community energy are how people get electricity.

In Europe and Canada, communities optimise an existing grid. In many countries of the Global South, microgrids and community-run systems are often the first access to electricity. The questions are close (sizing, data, indicators, governance, financing), so tools and lessons can travel in both directions. Azimut 360 would bring that experience.

Why it belongs in the Alliance

Same questions, different starting point

Planning, data capture, indicators and governance matter in both settings. A shared core that also fits off-grid and mini-grid cases is more robust.

Lessons travel both ways

Southern projects have long worked with local ownership, tight budgets and weak grids. Northern communities are now facing flexibility and resilience questions of the same kind.

A broader funding base

Development and climate funders support work that links North and South. A joint offer could reach lines that a purely European or Canadian one cannot (to be explored; nothing is committed).

What Azimut 360 brings

A Catalan cooperative founded in 2009, part of the social and solidarity economy network, with engineering and implementation of solar PV projects in Spain and abroad.

Rural microgrids

Strong specialisation in rural electrification with microgrids, including health and energy projects such as electricity for hospitals, schools and water.

Sub-Saharan experience

Work in countries such as Burkina Faso, Ivory Coast, Ghana, Mozambique, Uganda and Gambia, with first-hand knowledge of logistics, security and local capacity limits.

Community and cooperative models

Listed expertise in energy communities and cooperative models; a spin-off, Azimut O2, works on solar-powered medical oxygen.

Annex H · What the Alliance offers outside its members

Services that help everyone and compete with no one.

The test is simple: the Alliance does what no single member has a reason to do alone, and never delivers work a member could sell. Anything touching a client's own project, a specific site or a commercial product is out.

Start with four

1. A white paper

An annual state of local energy and digital twins: evidence across projects, how indicators are defined, a map of tools and gaps. Cheap, builds credibility and gives the Alliance its voice.

2. Case-record and indicator standard

A common record for community and district case studies, with agreed indicator definitions and reference calculations, so cases can be compared like with like.

3. Proposal desk

Watches calls, matches partners, offers consortium and reporting templates and a rotation rule. It does not write bids or pick winners.

4. Led and facilitated processes

A neutral convener, with a panel of experts from member teams as leaders, runs a multi-stakeholder process around one concrete project or place: workshops, shared diagnosis, working groups, documented outcome. We already do this (see the facilitation chapter). It takes more effort, so it starts with a few pilot processes.

The first three are cheap, show value within a year, and none touches a member's own business. Facilitation is the Alliance's signature activity and needs a funded coordinator.

Once trust exists

Benchmarking and validation

Run members' models against shared test datasets and publish the results. A neutral referee, not a consultant.

Procurement support

Model contract clauses and a checklist for cities buying digital twins, so buyers can ask for interoperability.

Training and technology transfer

Open courses, train-the-trainer material and adapted tools for partners in the Global South, built with local organisations and funded by development and climate lines where possible.
