01 · The problem

The obstacle is not effort. It is duplication and isolation.

Reviews keep finding the same pattern: tools and methods exist but are fragmented, hard to connect and rarely built for communities; start-ups lack expertise; evidence stays inside each project; and no one speaks for local energy systems with one voice. Projects also stall because the actors they depend on rarely sit at the same table.

Fragmented tools

Mature tools exist for design, but community-specific and interoperable ones are mostly prototypes.

Evidence stays local

Many ways to measure impact, none agreed, so lessons do not add up across projects.

No shared voice

Communities, cities and grid operators lobby separately, often on the same issues.

No neutral table

City, utility, financiers, developers and neighbours rarely meet around the same project, so it stalls between planning and implementation.

Based on our review of recent literature on energy community gaps (2021 to 2026).

02 · Why now

Policy, money and technology are lining up on the local level.

Three shifts make local energy the place where the transition is won or lost, and make the next few years a rare window to organise.

9 → 90 GW
Policy. The European Commission's 2026 Citizens Energy Package aims for roughly a tenfold rise in community-owned renewables by 2030. It is voluntary, so delivery depends on capacity on the ground.
8,000+
Scale. Energy communities counted in the EU by the Commission (an academic mapping counts about 3,900: definitions differ). Cities, regions and grid operators are moving too.
Twins
Technology and funding. EU programmes already fund digital twins on the grid side (e.g. TwinEU, DSO4DT) and community facilities separately. The bridge between them is missing.

Sources: European Commission, REScoop.eu, mapping study (Sustainability 2023), project sites. See references.

03 · The first engine

A new IEA EBC Annex is already on the table, supported by NRCan.

In early October 2026 we submitted a proposal to the IEA EBC Executive Committee for a new Annex: Enabling implementation of Community Energy Solutions through digital platforms. It moves the question from "can this work?" to "who will make it work?", using digital twins for fast feasibility and adding governance, financing and regulation to the model.

What it does

Governance models, financing, regulation and open-source digital-twin tools, brought together in one platform that guides a project from pre-feasibility to implementation.

How long

1 year of preparation, 3 of work and 1 of reporting, if the ExCo approves (decision expected at its November 2026 meeting).

04 · Many tools, no common foundations

Good tools exist. They do not share foundations.

We reviewed 15 tools and tool families for managing energy communities and local energy systems, from research projects, networks, public bodies and companies. Each has its own front end, back end, data model and indicators. For 11 of them we could not find the data model documented in public sources.

Different front ends are fine

Users differ, so interfaces should too.

Different foundations are costly

Data structure, data capture and indicators are rebuilt each time, so tools are hard to integrate and cases hard to compare.

A shared core would help

If a tool disappears or two tools join, data and indicators keep their meaning.

An alliance with four jobs.

08 · How it is run

Neutral by design, so competitors can sit at the same table.

A short charter

  • Open by default for the shared core
  • Members keep their proprietary work
  • No services that compete with members
  • Competition-law guidance for all meetings

Balanced bodies

  • Assembly of all members
  • Board balanced across the five member families
  • Technical committee for the commons
  • Small secretariat

Fair play on projects

  • Lead roles rotate with clear criteria
  • The Alliance facilitates, never picks winners
  • A share of won projects returns to the commons (if funders allow)

Legal form starts as a Spanish association (fast and low cost) and could move to an international non-profit when membership and funding justify it.

Catalonia and Quebec have had a cooperation agreement since 1996 (30 years in 2026), with a joint committee that sets a work programme every two years; climate was added in 2010 and research and innovation, including clean energy, are priorities. Idea to explore: ask for community energy and digital twins to be reflected in the next programme. Beyond that, a Canada-Spain-Europe axis connects IREC to REScoop.eu, Energy Cities and the IEA route.

Annex A · The IEA EBC Annex proposal

The Annex is the first engine: from "can it work?" to "who will make it work?"

Proposed title: Enabling implementation of Community Energy Solutions through digital platforms. The concept was submitted to the IEA EBC Executive Committee in early October 2026, supported by NRCan. Proposed lead and Operating Agent: Ursula Eicker (Concordia University).

The problem it addresses

Community-scale energy systems (solar PV energy communities, district heating and cooling, mixed-energy districts and campuses) are technically mature, yet uptake is slow. Feasibility studies answer "can this work?" but rarely "who will make this work?", so sound projects stall between planning and implementation. The Annex uses digital twins for fast technical and financial assessment and treats governance, participation, ownership, financing and regulation as part of the model.

Six objectives

1. Governance structures

Identify successful stakeholder roles, ownership and operating structures, utility engagement, participation and risk allocation.

2. The implementation gap

Find out why feasible projects do not advance, and build frameworks for institutional barriers, coordination, procurement and acceptance.

3. Financing

Business models, public-private and utility partnerships, community ownership and risk-sharing.

4. Regulation

Compare frameworks for district energy, energy communities, electricity sharing, tariffs, building design and zoning.

5. Digital planning tools

Apply and scale open-source digital-twin tools for demand simulation, community energy mapping, technology screening and life-cycle cost, with regulatory, financing and governance constraints built in.

6. Implementation roadmaps

Step-by-step guidance for municipalities, developers and citizen organisations, from feasibility study to operation.

Outputs

A governance and stakeholder framework, a participation and co-creation framework, a financing and business-model guidebook, a regulatory assessment, implementation roadmaps, demonstration case studies and policy recommendations, all integrated in one digital platform that guides the whole planning process.

Builds on earlier EBC work

Annex 51 (Energy Efficient Communities), Annex 63 (Implementation of Energy Strategies in Communities), Annex 73 (Net Zero Energy Resilient Public Communities), Annex 75 (Cost-effective Building Renovation at District Level) and Annex 83 (Positive Energy Districts). None of them combined fast digital feasibility assessment with the organisational delivery problem.

Timeline and partners

Duration

Preparation 1 year, working phase 3 years, reporting 1 year. Start depends on the ExCo decision.

Coordination

With the DHC, Cities, SHC, Energy Storage and Users (DSM) Technology Collaboration Programmes.

Outside collaborators proposed

Municipal organisations such as FCM, the Global Covenant of Mayors, C40 and Transition Accelerator organisations, and international bodies such as UN-Habitat and the OECD's Cities, Urban Policies and Sustainable Development Division.

How the Alliance relates to it

The Annex is government-led and time-limited. The Alliance would be its permanent, wider home: it keeps the shared core, the tools and the partners alive after the Annex ends, and lets companies and community networks take part, which an Annex cannot do.

Source: EBC Project Concept dated 24 August 2026. Open item: a Technology Readiness Assessment has not been carried out yet; the ExCo would ask for it at the workshop that develops the full proposal.

Annex B · Facilitation: method and track record

How the Alliance would run a facilitated process.

Local energy projects rarely fail because the technology does not work. They stall because the city, the utility, the financier, the developer and the neighbours have never worked out together who does what. A neutral convener closes that gap.

Track record

District heating at Laval and at Concordia

Our centre played the intermediary between the partners, ran workshops and worked out the different problems of the neighbours.

ABC Accelerator

Started in 2022 within the Montréal climate partnership. A "sustainability SWAT team" of seven kinds of actors: university, developer, financing, utility, engineering and architecture, law and the city. First test with two real developers.

CÔTÉ Habitat

A centre for the ecological transition of existing housing in Montréal, led by a housing network, a transition alliance and Concordia. Technical, social, urban and financial support in one process; two pilots planned to test the assumptions and the business models.

The ABC Accelerator in numbers

Estimated emission reductions for the two pilots: about 19% with the baseline proposals and 33% with the next-generation proposals (the project's own estimates, not measured results).

A process in six steps

1. Start from a concrete project

A real project or place, not an abstract topic.

2. Invite the whole chain

City, utility, financier, developer, engineers and architects, legal advice, and the community or neighbours.

3. Agree the rules first

Roles, confidentiality, what can be shared, and a fair way to select who takes part.

4. Listen and map

Workshops to understand each party's problems and constraints, and who is most and least open to change.

5. Work in small commissions

For example public space, energy systems, buildings and certification, with a monthly plenary.

6. Document and hand back

A diagnosis, proposals at two levels of ambition, ways to fund them and recommendations with costs and impacts, plus templates others can reuse.

What we learned

  • A university acting as a central, neutral agent is seen very positively and builds trust.
  • Innovative options scare people less when banks, municipalities and key actors visibly back them.
  • Examples from others, and a good pitch, speed up decisions.
  • Technical teams can start as the conservative force. Meeting after meeting, with their fears addressed, they became collaborative.
  • Three to four months of process help to smooth the first doubts.
  • Confidentiality worries come first: a non-disclosure agreement with the convener and side agreements with each partner were needed.
  • Free expert time does not allow deep dedication. The coordination needs funding: CÔTÉ plans a salaried lead from the start.

The line with what members do

The Alliance does
  • Convene, run workshops and facilitate
  • Map problems and agree the rules
  • Coordinate working groups and document the result
  • Turn what is learned into templates and tools open to all
The Alliance does not
  • Design the project or sell engineering
  • Take a commercial role in delivery
  • Choose vendors or winners
  • Replace the expertise members bring to the table

How it connects to the rest

Each process produces a real case for the shared case-record standard, evidence for the white paper and the Annex's governance work, and a pipeline of projects that are ready to be funded.

The shared core: five layers developed once, used by all

Not a platform and not a database: a common structure and vocabulary that any tool can read and write.

1. Geospatial and building metadata

Building identity and footprint, use, age, height, systems, coordinate reference, links to cadastre and open building datasets.

2. Socioeconomic structure

Households, ownership and membership, vulnerability, tariffs and costs, described with the same fields and codes.

3. Data capture and provenance

What is collected, how, by whom and when. Each value says if it was measured, modelled or declared, and where it came from.

4. Real-case records

A standard record for a community or district case study so that different projects can be compared like with like.

5. Key indicators

Agreed definitions, formulas, units and boundaries for the indicators, so numbers from different tools mean the same thing.

If a tool disappears

The data survives in a structure others can read, instead of being lost with the tool.

If two tools want to join

Integration is mapping to the same core once, not rewriting every connector.

If a community compares cases

Real cases are described with the same fields and indicators, so they can be compared.

If you want to keep your own approach

You keep your front end, back end and extras. Exchange within the association is optional.

Core versus yours

Core (shared, versioned, open): ontology, capture and provenance rules, indicator definitions, case-record format.

Yours: front ends, back ends, algorithms, local extras (with a clear prefix), and whatever you choose to exchange.

Annex F · What the Alliance offers outside its members

Services that help everyone and compete with no one.

The test is simple: the Alliance does what no single member has a reason to do alone, and never delivers work a member could sell. Anything touching a client's own project, a specific site or a commercial product is out.

Start with four

1. A white paper

An annual state of local energy and digital twins: evidence across projects, how indicators are defined, a map of tools and gaps. Cheap, builds credibility and gives the Alliance its voice.

2. Case-record and indicator standard

A common record for community and district case studies, with agreed indicator definitions and reference calculations, so cases can be compared like with like.

3. Proposal desk

Watches calls, matches partners, offers consortium and reporting templates and a rotation rule. It does not write bids or pick winners.

4. Facilitated processes

A neutral convener runs a multi-stakeholder process around one concrete project or place: workshops, shared diagnosis, working groups, documented outcome. We already do this (see the facilitation chapter). It takes more effort, so it starts with a few pilot processes.

The first three are cheap, show value within a year, and none touches a member's own business. Facilitation is the Alliance's signature activity and needs a funded coordinator.

Once trust exists

Benchmarking and validation

Run members' models against shared test datasets and publish the results. A neutral referee, not a consultant.

Procurement support

Model contract clauses and a checklist for cities buying digital twins, so buyers can ask for interoperability.
